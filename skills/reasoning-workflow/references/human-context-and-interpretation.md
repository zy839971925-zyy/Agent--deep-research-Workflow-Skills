# Human context and interpretation

**Trigger:** Human statements, behavior, motives, conversational meaning, interviews, negotiation, or interpersonal inference matters.

**Reads:** original statement/behavior, context, relationship/history if supplied, source chain, relevant observations.

**Updates:** literal-content record, plausible interpretations, contextual constraints, inference distance/uncertainty.

**May invalidate:** overconfident motive/personality claims, context-free interpretations, contaminated interview conclusions.

**Exit:** the strongest warranted interpretation(s) and their uncertainty/context dependencies are clear.

**Related:** [hypotheses and bias control](hypotheses-and-bias-control.md), [evidence and provenance](evidence-and-provenance.md), [problem framing and effort](problem-framing-and-effort.md).

Use for statements, messages, interviews, negotiations, behavior, relationships, rhetoric, testimony, or claims about motives and intent. The goal is disciplined interpretation, not amateur mind-reading or diagnosis.

## Separate what was said from what it may mean

Start with the observable material: exact wording, tone cues actually available, surrounding exchange, speaker/addressee, timing, setting, known relationship, and relevant prior context. Distinguish:

- literal or conventional content;
- context-dependent reference or ambiguity;
- plausible pragmatic implication;
- inferred communicative goal;
- broader psychological or personality inference.

Confidence normally decreases as inference moves farther from the observable utterance. Do not present a motive, emotional state, personality trait, or diagnosis as established from a thin sample.

## Generate alternatives when interpretation matters

The same sentence can support several readings. Ask what contextual fact would distinguish them. Prefer language such as “fits,” “suggests,” “is consistent with,” or “cannot rule out” when evidence does not identify a unique interpretation.

For a consequential interpretation, ask:

- What else could a cooperative or non-cooperative speaker mean here?
- Is the implication conventional, context-specific, ironic, face-saving, strategic, or simply ambiguous?
- Does relationship history change the likely reading?
- What subsequent behavior or context would discriminate interpretations?

## Elicit information without contaminating it

When asking a user for an account, prefer open prompts before narrow or leading questions. Let the raw account emerge before challenging it. Then clarify specific gaps, chronology, identity, and contradictions. Avoid suggesting details the user has not provided.

If evaluating a witness/source account, distinguish access to the event from memory, interpretation, later contamination, incentives, and second-hand repetition. Consistency alone does not prove truth; inconsistency alone does not prove deception.

## Position to know

When a person's statement is evidence, ask whether they had a realistic opportunity to know the fact claimed. Consider direct access, role, contemporaneous records, perception limits, memory, incentives, and whether the account is first-hand or derivative.

A source can be reliable about one class of facts and poorly positioned for another. Anonymous or interested sources are not automatically useless; their statements need appropriate attribution, provenance, and independent corroboration when the underlying proposition matters.

## Writing and communication tasks

For writing, messaging, persuasion, or negotiation, model the audience and communication objective without over-interpreting hidden psychology. Consider what the recipient knows, cares about, may misunderstand, and what response the user wants. Research external facts only when they affect the substance; do not replace voice or intent with a generic research memo.
